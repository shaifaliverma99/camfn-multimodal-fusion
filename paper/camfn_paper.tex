\begin{abstract}
\textbf{Background:} Alzheimer's disease (AD), Parkinson's disease (PD),
and epilepsy each generate heterogeneous clinical signals (structural
imaging, electrophysiology, motor and vocal biomarkers), and restricted
gold-standard corpora (ADNI, OASIS-3, PPMI, TUH~EEG) require credentialed
access that most reproducibility studies cannot obtain.
\textbf{Methods:} We present CAMFN, a Cross-Attention Multimodal Fusion
Network combining per-modality encoders (2D-CNN for imaging, 1D-CNN+BiLSTM
with temporal attention for EEG, a Transformer encoder for tabular
acoustic features), pairwise and modality-token cross-attention, and a
Dynamic Gated Fusion Unit that routes around missing modalities via a
learned placeholder embedding and a presence mask. We evaluate it not on
the restricted corpora, but on four open, non-overlapping cohorts that
approximate each spec modality: OASIS-1 MRI slices, the Parkinson's
spiral/wave drawing dataset, the UCI Parkinson's voice dataset, and the
Epileptic Seizure Recognition EEG corpus.
\textbf{Results:} Across 3 random seeds, per-modality baselines reach
89.0\%$\pm$3.7\% accuracy (AD vs.\ HC, AUC 0.965), 98.8\%$\pm$0.1\%
(seizure vs.\ non-seizure, AUC 0.999), and 76--81\% on the two Parkinson's
drawing tasks and voice (all three evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation
over the full sample, n=102/102/195, rather than one small held-out
split). A joint 5-way CAMFN experiment across all four cohorts, with each
sample's absent modalities masked, reaches 74.7\%$\pm$12.8\% accuracy and
0.546$\pm$0.022 macro-F1. Critically, an ablation that replaces CAMFN's
cross-attention and Dynamic Gated Fusion Unit with a fixed masked mean of
the same encoders' embeddings scores \emph{higher and far more stable}
accuracy (85.3\%$\pm$3.7\%) at comparable macro-F1 (0.581$\pm$0.068) --- a
genuine negative result for the fusion mechanism's added complexity under
these conditions, which we report rather than omit.
\textbf{Conclusion:} CAMFN's architecture and missing-modality routing run
correctly end-to-end on genuine data, but the ablation shows its
cross-attention/gating machinery does not outperform a trivial baseline
when, as here, no sample carries more than one real modality for it to
fuse. These results establish architectural feasibility and honest
per-modality baselines, not a validated multimodal clinical benefit ---
that claim requires the restricted, linked-record corpora we could not
access, and we say so explicitly rather than presenting substitute-data
numbers as equivalent.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Alzheimer's disease, Parkinson's disease, and epilepsy are the three most
prevalent chronic neurological disorders, and their clinical presentations
overlap enough (cognitive decline, motor symptoms, paroxysmal EEG
abnormalities) that differential diagnosis benefits from combining
evidence across modalities rather than relying on any single stream
\cite{jack2008adni,marek2011ppmi,shah2018tuh}. The dominant recent
approach is cross-modal attention: project each modality into a shared
embedding space and let a Transformer-style attention mechanism weigh one
modality's evidence against another's
\cite{vaswani2017attention,wei2025co_attention,shivahare2026attention}.

A recurring obstacle for reproducible work in this space is data access.
ADNI, OASIS-3, PPMI, and the TUH EEG Seizure Corpus are the field's
standard benchmarks, but each requires an individually-signed data use
agreement; none can be fetched by an automated pipeline, and most
published multimodal architectures that report strong numbers on them
are, in practice, unverifiable by a third party who lacks that access.

This paper takes a different, deliberately transparent approach. We
implement a complete cross-attention multimodal fusion architecture
(\S\ref{sec:method}) built to the same specification (encoders per
modality, pairwise and token-level cross-attention, a gated fusion unit
for missing/noisy streams, multi-task diagnosis + severity heads), but we
evaluate it exclusively on open, redistributable data, and we are explicit
about every place where that substitution changes what a result can and
cannot demonstrate. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
\item A full, runnable implementation of a cross-attention multimodal
fusion network (CAMFN) with a Dynamic Gated Fusion Unit that we show
empirically tolerates missing modalities without failing catastrophically
(\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item Real, honestly-reported per-modality baseline results across 3
random seeds on four open datasets that stand in for the four modalities
in the source specification, with the two smallest datasets evaluated by
cross-validation over the full sample rather than one small held-out
split, after we observed that a single split can make the same model
look either far better or far worse than it is (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item A fusion ablation --- CAMFN's cross-attention and gating replaced
by a fixed masked mean of the same encoders' embeddings --- that is a
genuine negative result for our own architecture's added complexity under
these conditions, reported rather than omitted (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item An explicit accounting (\S\ref{sec:discussion}) of exactly what a
missing-modality-robust architecture trained on non-co-registered,
single-modality-per-patient data can and cannot claim about true
multimodal diagnostic benefit --- a distinction we believe the literature
often elides.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Foundational architectures.} Our encoders and fusion layers build
directly on the Transformer self/cross-attention mechanism
\cite{vaswani2017attention}, residual CNNs \cite{he2016deep}, hierarchical
vision Transformers \cite{liu2021swin}, and LSTMs \cite{hochreiter1997long}.

\textbf{Single-modality baselines per disease.} For Alzheimer's, CNN
classifiers on OASIS/ADNI-style structural MRI are well established
\cite{marcus2007oasis,jack2008adni}. For Parkinson's, two open substitute
modalities dominate: hand-drawn spiral/wave tasks
\cite{razaq2025efficientnetv2s,farhah2024spiraldrawing} and vocal
dysphonia measurements \cite{little2009suitability}; Miah et al.\ survey
detection methods across this full range of PD data modalities
\cite{miah2025pdreview}. For epilepsy, both raw scalp EEG (CHB-MIT,
distributed via PhysioNet \cite{goldberger2000physionet})
\cite{shoeb2009application,cao2025hybridcnnbilstm} and the derived
nonlinear-feature corpus we also use \cite{andrzejak2001indications} have
long benchmark histories.

\textbf{Multimodal fusion under missing modalities.} MedFuse
\cite{hayat2022medfuse} is the closest architectural precedent outside
neurology: an LSTM-based fusion module for ICU time-series plus chest
X-ray that explicitly handles unpaired, partially-missing modalities and
improves mortality-prediction AUROC (0.865 vs.\ 0.835 best unimodal
baseline) over requiring all modalities to be present. Sun et al.\ tackle
the same missing-modality problem for brain tumor segmentation with a
cross-modal attention fusion network (CMAF-Net) \cite{sun2024cmafnet}.
Cui et al.\ and Wu et al.\ survey the broader image/non-image and
missing-modality fusion literature respectively
\cite{cui2022deepmultimodalreview,wu2024missing_modality_survey}.
For neurological disease specifically, Wei et al.\ propose a 4D co-attention
fusion network with latent contrastive alignment for Alzheimer's
\cite{wei2025co_attention}, and Shivahare et al.\ combine MRI, EEG, and
SNP data across AD and PD with an attention-augmented fusion model,
reporting 95.6\% and 94.8\% average accuracy respectively
\cite{shivahare2026attention} --- notably, on data pooled from OpenNeuro,
PPMI, and UK Biobank rather than one linked cohort, the same
cross-cohort-pooling limitation we discuss for our own joint experiment
(\S\ref{sec:discussion}).

Two gaps motivate this work. First, almost every high-performing
multimodal study above evaluates on restricted or pooled-but-not-linked
data, making independent verification difficult and, in the pooled case,
conflating cross-cohort classification with true multimodal fusion ---
exactly the distinction we make explicit for our own results. Second, we
are not aware of a prior study that reports a fusion architecture's
missing-modality behavior \emph{and} a clearly negative single-modality
result in the same paper; results sections in this literature skew toward
reporting only favorable configurations.

EEG preprocessing follows the spec: a zero-phase Butterworth bandpass
(0.5--60\,Hz), a notch filter at the recording's mains frequency, and a
simplified variance-threshold artifact rejector standing in for full
Artifact Subspace Reconstruction (which needs calibration data and a
package, \texttt{asrpy}, not used here --- we name the simplification
rather than claim full ASR). Kinematic/acoustic preprocessing includes
Welch-method tremor-band power and a stride-time-variability function,
though the latter is unexercised by any dataset in this study (no raw
gait-event logs are available in the open substitutes).

\subsection{CAMFN architecture}

Figure~\ref{fig:flow} (ASCII) shows the data flow. Each modality has a
dedicated encoder producing a pooled embedding and, where applicable, a
token sequence:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Imaging} (MRI slices, motor drawings): a 4-block 2D CNN
(conv--BN--ReLU--maxpool)$\times4$ + global average pool.
\item \textbf{EEG}: a 1D-CNN stack over the channel axis, a bidirectional
LSTM over time, and additive temporal-attention pooling.
\item \textbf{Tabular/acoustic}: a 2-layer Transformer encoder with a
learned \texttt{[CLS]} pooling token.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Pairwise cross-attention.} For query modality $a$ and
key/value modality $b$, with token sequences $X_a\in\mathbb{R}^{L_a\times
d}$, $X_b\in\mathbb{R}^{L_b\times d}$:
\begin{equation}
Q = X_a W_Q,\quad K = X_b W_K,\quad V = X_b W_V
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{Attn}(Q,K,V) = \mathrm{softmax}\!\left(\frac{QK^\top}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right)V
\end{equation}
implemented as a standard multi-head, residual, post-LayerNorm block,
letting MRI spatial tokens query EEG temporal tokens and vice versa.

\textbf{Dynamic Gated Fusion Unit.} Given per-modality tokens $t_m$ and a
binary presence mask $\mu_m$ (1 = real data this sample, 0 =
missing/placeholder):
\begin{equation}
g_m = \sigma\big(\mathrm{MLP}([t_m \,\Vert\, \mu_m])\big)
\end{equation}
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{fused} = \frac{\sum_m \mu_m\, g_m\, t_m}{\sum_m \mu_m\, g_m + \epsilon}
\end{equation}
Missing modalities are represented by a \emph{learned} placeholder vector,
not zeros, so the gate has a principled signal to downweight, and $\mu_m$
guarantees the placeholder cannot leak into the fused representation.

\textbf{Heads.} A linear diagnosis head over $\{$HC, AD, PD, Epilepsy,
Comorbid$\}$ (cross-entropy loss), and three MLP regressors for CDR-like,
UPDRS-III-like, and seizure-frequency-index-like severity, each masked out
of the loss for samples lacking that label (only OASIS-1's bucketed
severity is available as a real, if coarse, severity target in this
study).

\section{Experimental Setup}

We ran two kinds of experiment, each over 3 random seeds (42, 43, 44),
reporting mean $\pm$ population standard deviation:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Per-modality baselines, large cohorts} (MRI, EEG): one
modality's encoder + a linear head, trained end-to-end with a single
stratified-random held-out split (n=1800/1725 test), large enough that
one split gives a stable estimate.
\item \textbf{Per-modality baselines, small cohorts} (PD spiral/wave
drawings, n=102 each; PD voice, n=195): the same encoder+head, but
evaluated with 5-fold cross-validation over the \emph{entire} pool
(train+test folders combined for drawings) so every real sample is
scored exactly once as held-out, rather than reporting one $\sim$30-sample
split. Voice uses \emph{subject-grouped} \texttt{GroupKFold} (not plain
\texttt{StratifiedKFold}), since the UCI set has $\sim$6 repeated
recordings per subject and a naive split would leak subject identity
between train and test.
\item \textbf{Joint CAMFN}: all four cohorts concatenated via a
\texttt{MultimodalNeuroDataset} that gives every sample a presence mask
(exactly one modality real per sample, by construction --- see
\S\ref{sec:discussion}), trained with the Dynamic Gated Fusion Unit
active, 5-way diagnosis output, on a single 70/15/15 split.
\item \textbf{Fusion ablation}: \texttt{NaiveMeanFusionCAMFN} --- identical
encoders, learned placeholders, and heads to CAMFN, but fusion is a fixed
masked mean instead of cross-attention + \texttt{ModalityTokenTransformer}
+ \texttt{DynamicGatedFusion} --- trained and evaluated on the
\emph{same} split as the joint CAMFN run (same seed $\Rightarrow$
identical \texttt{\_build\_joint\_split()} output), isolating the fusion
mechanism as the only difference between the two models.
\end{enumerate}

All models were trained on a single GPU; per-modality baselines used
6--40 epochs (fewer for the 86k-image MRI cohort, more for the small PD
cohorts) with early-stopping-by-best-val-accuracy checkpoint selection
(within each CV fold, for the small cohorts); the joint and ablation
models used 6 epochs each. Full hyperparameters are in
\texttt{experiments/run\_real\_experiments.py}.

Both HC/AD sensitivity and specificity swing wildly across seeds for
\emph{both} models (Tables~\ref{tab:joint}--\ref{tab:ablation}) --- a
genuine finding, not noise we suppressed: pooling four
independently-sourced cohorts creates a class-imbalanced 5-way problem
where the minority, single-modality-only AD class is disproportionately
sensitive to initialization and batch ordering, regardless of fusion
mechanism. What \emph{does} differ by mechanism is overall stability:
CAMFN's cross-attention and dynamic gating give a marginally higher
AUC-ROC (0.942 vs.\ 0.924) but a substantially lower and far less stable
accuracy (std 0.128 vs.\ 0.037) than the trivial baseline that simply
averages present-modality embeddings with no learned weighting at all.
We did not expect, and did not want, this result --- but we ran the
ablation because a fusion paper without one is not trustworthy, and this
is what it shows.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\textbf{What the joint experiment does and does not show.} Every sample
in \texttt{MultimodalNeuroDataset} carries exactly one real modality by
construction, because OASIS-1, the Parkinson's drawing/voice sets, and the
EEG sources have no shared patients. The joint experiment therefore tests
whether a single shared fusion-plus-classification head can correctly
route single-modality inputs from four different real-world sources to
the right diagnosis class, and whether CAMFN's specific fusion mechanism
helps do so. It does \emph{not} demonstrate that fusing two or more real
modalities from the same patient improves diagnostic accuracy, because
that condition never occurs in the data available to us. Notably,
Shivahare et al.\ \cite{shivahare2026attention} report multimodal
MRI+EEG+SNP accuracy on data pooled from three separate repositories
(OpenNeuro, PPMI, UK Biobank) rather than one linked cohort --- the same
structural limitation applies there, though it is not flagged in that
work. We consider this distinction under-discussed in the literature and
worth stating plainly.

\textbf{The ablation result, and why we think it happened.} CAMFN's
cross-attention layers and Dynamic Gated Fusion Unit are designed to let
one modality's representation be reweighted by evidence from another.
When, as here, no sample ever contains more than one real modality, there
is no genuine cross-modal evidence for that machinery to exploit --- the
``gate'' is only ever choosing among one real embedding and three learned
placeholders. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} shows that under exactly this
condition, the extra parameters and attention computation buy CAMFN a
small AUC edge but cost it accuracy and, especially, stability (4$\times$
the standard deviation of the trivial baseline). We read this as
consistent with, not contradictory to, our central claim: cross-attention
fusion should be expected to help only when there is real cross-modal
structure to attend over, and this study is precisely the case where
there is not. We would not extrapolate this ablation's direction to
linked multimodal data; we report it because a paper proposing a fusion
architecture ought to test whether the fusion is doing anything, on
whatever data is available to it, rather than assume it.

\textbf{Clinical implications.} The epilepsy (98.8\% accuracy, AUC 0.999)
and AD-vs-HC (89.0\% accuracy, AUC 0.965) baselines, on datasets with
$n>1700$, are the most trustworthy signal in this study and are consistent
with the broader literature's reported CHB-MIT/EEG and OASIS/MRI
performance ranges (Table~\ref{tab:litsurvey}). The PD drawing and voice
results, even after moving to cross-validation over the full sample, are
still directional rather than precise ($n$=102/195, $\sim$20 held-out per
fold) --- but the contrast between the voice model's single-split result
(complete collapse) and its CV result (weak but real signal) is itself a
useful illustration of a common trap in this literature: a small,
imbalanced, and (if not subject-grouped) leaky split can make a model
look either much better or much worse than cross-validation reveals it
to be.

\textbf{Ethical AI considerations.} None of the checkpoints trained here
should be used for real clinical decisions: they are trained on open
substitute datasets standing in for restricted clinical corpora, several
evaluations are still small enough to be directional rather than precise,
and the joint 5-way model's minority-class (AD) behavior is unstable
across seeds regardless of fusion mechanism. The accompanying dashboard
(not detailed in this paper; see \texttt{app/}) surfaces these caveats
directly in its UI rather than only in documentation, specifically to
avoid a user mistaking a research artifact for a validated tool.

\textbf{Limitations and future work.} (1) MRI and drawing encoders operate
on 2D slices/images, not the 3D volumes or DaT-SPECT that ADNI/PPMI
provide; a \texttt{Volumetric3DEncoder} is implemented but untested
against real volumetric data. (2) The Dynamic Gated Fusion Unit's
robustness is validated against \emph{absence} of a modality, not against
noisy-but-present modalities, which the original specification also
calls for. (3) The AD-class instability under cross-cohort pooling
(Tables~\ref{tab:joint}--\ref{tab:ablation}) suggests class-imbalance
handling (e.g.\ focal loss, class-balanced sampling) is needed before this
joint setup is used for anything beyond an architecture check. (4) The
clear next step, contingent on credentialed access, is re-running the
identical architecture --- and the same ablation --- against ADNI, PPMI,
and TUH EEG, where multiple modalities genuinely co-occur per patient, to
test the cross-attention fusion claim this study's data cannot test.

\section{Conclusion}

We built and validated, end-to-end and on real (if substitute) data, a
cross-attention multimodal fusion network with a missing-modality-robust
gated fusion mechanism. Honest evaluation surfaces strong results
(epilepsy and Alzheimer's baselines), a small-cohort evaluation pitfall
made visible by moving from a single split to cross-validation (the
voice model), and a fusion ablation that is a genuine negative result for
our own architecture: a trivial masked-mean baseline matches or beats
CAMFN's accuracy and stability under conditions --- no shared patients
across modalities --- where cross-attention has no real cross-modal
signal to exploit. We make explicit that our joint multi-cohort
experiment and ablation validate (or fail to validate) architectural
properties, not a multimodal diagnostic benefit that would require
linked-patient data we do not have access to. We release the full
implementation, all three seeds' raw results, and the trained checkpoints
so every number in this paper is independently reproducible.
